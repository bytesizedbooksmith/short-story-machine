Good Hooks

The hook is your first page. In a short story, there's no time to warm up: by the bottom of page one, a reader should want page two.

The machine asks you to choose your opening while planning (Step 4) and to read it before anything else is written (Step 8). This page helps with both.

By the End of Page One, Your Reader Has

  1. A person: your main character, already doing or saying something.
  2. A situation: something specific happening right now, not a routine.
  3. Something off: a problem, a request, a surprise, anything that raises a question.
  4. The genre: the ghost, the spark, the body, the magic. If it's fantasy, the reader knows by now.
  5. The voice: the narrator's personality, from the first line.

What they don't need yet: backstory, how the magic works, or why the shop exists.

Ways In That Usually Work

Way inFor example
The arrivalSomeone walks in with the problem. "Do you fix curses that are technically working as intended?"
The genre misbehavingThe ghost, spell or gadget does something it shouldn't, right away.
Mid-problemThings have already gone wrong. The reader catches up as they go.
A line that demands a replyA question or demand the reader wants answered.
A contradictionI'd planned for everything. Then the client said no.

Openings That Usually Stall

  • Preparing, tidying, arranging, or waiting for the story to start
  • Waking up, the weather, the view, or a character alone with their thoughts
  • Backstory or worldbuilding before anything happens
  • Explaining how the magic or the rules work
  • Characters agreeing on procedure or permission instead of talking like people
The quick test: read only the first page and ask, if I picked this up in a shop, would I turn the page? If the honest answer is "maybe," it isn't a hook yet.

If the Opening Isn't Working

Say so plainly at Step 8: "not hooked," "too slow," "too much explaining." If you turn the opening down twice, the machine stops patching and offers you three completely different first pages to choose from. A fresh direction usually beats a fifth round of small fixes.
