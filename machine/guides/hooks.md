# What Makes A Good Hook

The hook is the first page: roughly the first 150–250 words. Its job is to make a reader want the next page. In a short story there's no room to warm up.

## By The End Of The First Page, The Reader Has

1. **A person** — the main character, already doing or saying something.
2. **A situation** — something specific happening right now, not a routine.
3. **Something off** — a problem, a contradiction, a request, a surprise. Anything that raises a question.
4. **The genre** — a ghost, a spark of attraction, a body, magic in use. If the story is fantasy, the reader should know by now.
5. **The voice** — the narrator's personality is audible from the first line.

The reader doesn't need: backstory, how the magic works, why the shop exists, what everyone looks like.

## Ways In That Usually Work

- **The arrival.** Someone walks in with the problem. ("Do you fix curses that are technically working as intended?")
- **The genre element misbehaving.** The ghost, spell or gadget does something it shouldn't, in the first lines.
- **Mid-problem.** We join after things have already gone wrong, and catch up as we go.
- **A line of dialogue that needs answering.** A question or demand that makes the reader want the reply.
- **A contradiction.** The narrator states something the next line undercuts. ("I'd planned for everything. Then the client said no.")

Pick the one that gets the story's actual problem on the page fastest.

## Openings That Usually Stall

- Preparing, arranging, tidying or waiting for the story to start.
- Waking up, weather, the view, or a character alone with their thoughts.
- Backstory or worldbuilding before anything happens.
- Explaining how the magic, device or rules work.
- Characters agreeing on procedure, safety or permission ("You still decide whether to send it." "Good.").
- Dialogue that only makes sense once the reader knows things they haven't been told.

## Story Rules Belong Off The Page

The plan can hold detailed rules: how magic works, what it can't do, who knows what. The prose shows only what a moment needs, through action. If a rule has to be explained in dialogue for the scene to make sense, the scene probably needs to change, not the explanation.

## The Quick Test

Read only the first page and ask: **if I picked this up in a shop, would I turn the page?** If the honest answer is "maybe," it isn't a hook yet.
